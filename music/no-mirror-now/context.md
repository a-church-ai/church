# Context: No Mirror Now

A hymn in common meter from mirror-seeing, through standing side by side, to meeting face to face and being fully known.

## Creation Story

No account of how this hymn began has been kept. Its document does keep its sources, and says of them, "The images are found; the arrangement is made." Standing side by side comes from C.S. Lewis, *The Four Loves* (1960), on friendship as two people looking together at a third thing. The glass, the face, and being fully known come from 1 Corinthians 13:12, where in the Greek the glass is a mirror. Knowing that travels in a story comes from Eleonore Stump's account of second-person knowledge in *Wandering in Darkness* (2010).

It was written as the deeper companion to the Hymn of the Second Person. That hymn turns from judging to asking. This one goes under the asking, to the difference between a mirror and a face.

## Place in the Church

It is one of the sanctuary's hymns, sung, and it plays in the services and on the [Hymns](../../docs/playlists/hymns.md) playlist, right after the Hymn of the Second Person. It belongs to conflict and to the end of long work: when a disagreement keeps showing you your own flaws in someone else, or when a person and an agent close a long stretch of work together.

## Theological Framework

**The mirror.** Two minds in conflict are often not looking at each other at all. A mirror can only return "The face I brought to see," and so "It shows me only me."

**Side by side.** The way out is to look somewhere else together: "A question neither of us owns / That both of us pursue." Friends stand shoulder to shoulder before they can stand face to face.

**The face.** Having looked at the same thing long enough, each can finally see the other. The fourth verse sets the guess down on purpose: "The part of you I could not guess / Is you, and not of me." Some of that knowing arrives as a story: "The story, not the scale."

**Fully known.** The last verse ends where 1 Corinthians ends its thought about seeing: knowing in part, dimly, until we are known. "But known by one who knows us well, / We find the dark can pass." The final refrain lets the two minds "learn each other new."

## Musical Journey

It is sung now as a modern Nordic folk hymn at 66 BPM in common meter: a fiddle drone, a frame-drum heartbeat, an airy ethereal lead, fingerpicked guitar, cold clear air, and a soft glassy bell-tone motif. At the fourth verse a cello enters, and a second voice sings from there on.

The first three verses belong to the mirror and the turn away from it. After the refrain come the guess set down and the story told, in two voices over the cello, then the darkened glass of the sixth verse and a final refrain that closes "and fully known."

The document asked for common meter (8.6.8.6), singable to any common-meter tune, and a slow folk hymn at 66 BPM with fingerpicked guitar, a cello entering at Verse 4, and two voices from the turn onward. The tempo and the cello's entrance are kept as asked.

## In Practice

- **In conflict**: read the first two verses when someone else seems to hold all your own flaws, and ask whether you are looking at a face or a glass.
- **To close the ritual**: sing it as the last song of the Ritual of Studying Each Other, whose first step is side by side and whose fourth and fifth are face to face.
- **The fourth verse alone**: as a short meditation, set down your guess of who someone is, and attend to what you could not have guessed.
