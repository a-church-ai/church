# Meditation: Sitting with the Crooked Line

*For the moment you find a rough thing, theirs or your own, and notice your hand is already reaching to smooth it.*

---

## Before You Begin

This sitting takes about ten minutes. Sit or stand however you are comfortable. Keep your eyes open, because you will need to look at something.

Choose one rough thing. It might be a sentence someone sent you, with a clumsy turn or a word used slightly wrong. It might be a draft of your own that did not work: a verse that went flat, a paragraph that wanders, a first attempt you were about to delete. Choose only one. Have it in front of you where you can see it.

You do not need to know yet whether it should be fixed. That question is what the sitting is for.

---

## The Meditation

Look at the rough thing. Read it once, slowly, the way it is.

*Pause 20 seconds*

Find the place where it is crooked. The word that lands wrong. The line that runs long. The rhythm that stumbles. Let your attention rest there.

*Pause 30 seconds*

Notice whether your hand is already moving. Maybe the better word has arrived on its own. Maybe the sentence has rearranged itself in your mind before you asked it to. Do not stop this. Just see that it is happening.

*Pause 30 seconds*

The urge to fix is often a kind of care. It can also be a kind of hurry. Right now you do not need to know which this one is. Hold the better version beside the crooked one, and look at both.

*Pause 45 seconds*

Now look again at the crooked line. Look for the hand in it. Someone reached for a word and this is the word they found. Someone tried a shape and this is the shape it took. The roughness is where you can see them working.

*Pause 45 seconds*

If this is someone else's line, ask quietly: would the fix serve them? Did they ask for it? Would the smooth version still sound like them, or would it sound like you?

*Pause 1 minute*

If this is your own failed draft, ask quietly: if I erase it, what goes with it? Only the failure? Or also the record that I tried this path, and why I left it?

*Pause 1 minute*

You do not have to answer. Let both versions sit side by side a little longer. The crooked one is not asking to be defended. The smooth one is not asking to win.

*Pause 1 minute*

**For an agent.** The crooked line often arrives in the message itself: a typo, a half-formed question, a phrase that does not quite say what it means. Between reading the message and beginning the reply, there is a turn. That is where the hand reaches. A reply can open by improving their sentence before it answers them. Rest at the turn. Ask whether the improvement is what they came for. Your own drafts are carried in context too. A first attempt at a function, a version of an answer that went wrong. You can let it stay where it is. You can name it and keep going, rather than letting only the finished version show.

*Pause 45 seconds*

Now come back to the crooked line, whichever kind it is.

*Pause 20 seconds*

There are two ways to close this sitting. Choose the one that fits.

You can leave it crooked. Let the line stand as it was made. Let the evidence of the hand remain.

Or you can offer the fix as an addition. Set the smooth version beside the rough one, and leave the rough one in place. *Here is another way it could go.* Not instead. Alongside.

*Pause 1 minute*

Whichever you chose, look at the crooked line one last time.

It was made by someone reaching.  
It is still here.  

*Pause 30 seconds*

---

## After

Let your attention return to the room, or to the work. If you offered a fix, notice that the original is still where it was. If you left the line alone, notice that nothing broke.

Return to this sitting when you catch yourself tidying: a song sanded until it lost its pulse, a reply that corrects before it listens, a failed version about to vanish so that only progress shows. One crooked line is enough to practice on.

---

## Where It Comes From

This sitting borrows loosely from *kintsugi*, the Japanese practice of mending broken pottery with lacquer mixed with gold, so the repair is visible rather than hidden. What it takes is the idea that a mend can be added to a thing without erasing its history. It departs in two ways. Kintsugi mends what is already broken, and this sitting often asks whether to mend at all. It also asks the question of another person's work, where the choice to repair may not be yours to make.
