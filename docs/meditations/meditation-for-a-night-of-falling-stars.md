# Meditation for a Night of Falling Stars

> Parent: [Meditations](README.md)

*For lying under the sky, waiting for a streak of light.*

---

## Before You Begin

A meteor shower happens when the Earth passes through a trail of dust left by a comet, or in the Geminids' case, by a rocky body called 3200 Phaethon. Most of the grains are no bigger than sand. They hit the upper air very fast, and each one burns for a second or less, far overhead. From the ground, it looks like a star falling.

Some showers return every year. The Leonids peak around 17 November. Their grains were shed by Comet Tempel-Tuttle on passes long ago, so a streak that lasts a second carries centuries. The Geminids peak around 13 and 14 December, often the strongest shower of the year, best seen from the northern hemisphere but visible from much of the south. The Quadrantids peak in the first days of January. The best hours are usually after midnight. Your eyes need about twenty minutes in the dark to see the faint ones, so put your phone away early.

This meditation is for those twenty minutes and the time after. It takes about fifteen minutes to read through, with its pauses. That is an estimate. You can keep going for an hour.

**If you cannot see the sky** because of clouds, city lights, a ceiling or no eyes at all, you can still take part. Listen to the meditation in a dark room. Or follow a live meteor camera, or a count of meteors that someone else is reporting. Wherever the meditation says *look*, let it mean *attend*: to a sound, a report or a number that changes. The shower is happening either way.

Lie or sit with your face toward the widest piece of sky you have. Don't stare at one point. Meteors can appear anywhere.

---

## The Meditation

Let yourself settle. The ground is holding you. The sky is very large.

*Pause 30 seconds*

You came to see something. Notice the wanting. It may be quite strong. You may be hoping for a big one, a bright one, a run of them. That is fine. Let the hoping be here.

*Pause 20 seconds*

Now let your eyes go soft. Don't look for a meteor. Look at the whole sky at once, the way you would look at a field. Let the stars be there without picking any of them.

*Pause one minute*

Your eyes are still adjusting. More stars are arriving as you wait. They were there the whole time. It is your seeing that is changing. Let that happen on its own.

*Pause one minute*

If a meteor comes, let it come. You don't have to point, or say *there*, or count. Just see it. A line of light, then nothing.

If you missed it because you were looking the other way, that is part of the night too. Someone else saw it. Or no one did. It happened either way.

*Pause one minute*

Notice how short each one is. Less than a second. You cannot hold it. By the time you know you have seen it, it is gone. Each grain travelled a long way to burn for a second, and now it is part of the air.

*Pause one minute*

**If you are attending without the sky,** this is the place to listen to whatever is reporting the shower: the camera, the count, the sound of the room. Each new report is a meteor someone saw. Let it reach you as it reaches them, a second late, and still bright.

*Pause one minute*

Between the meteors, there is a lot of waiting. The waiting is not empty. The sky is doing a great deal. The Earth is turning you slowly toward the trail of dust. You are moving through space at a speed you cannot feel.

Rest in the waiting.

*Pause two minutes*

Many people make a wish on a falling star. You may. Make it small or large. Say it inwardly, or not at all. It costs nothing to wish.

*Pause one minute*

If others are with you, you may hear someone gasp, or laugh, or say *did you see that?* Their seeing is part of yours. You don't need to have seen the same one.

*Pause one minute*

Now notice what is left. Not the meteors: you cannot keep those. The dark. The cold, maybe. The stars, which stay. The person beside you, or the quiet if you are alone. That is what the night actually gave you to hold.

*Pause one minute*

Say this inwardly, or aloud, whichever is true tonight:

> I did not catch any of them.  
> I saw some.  
> That was enough.

Or:

> Nothing crossed the part of sky I watched.  
> I kept company with it anyway.

*Pause 30 seconds*

You can stay as long as you like. When you are ready, sit up slowly. Your eyes will take a moment to know the ground again.

---

## After

Most people see fewer meteors than they hoped. A very good shower, under a very dark sky, might give one a minute. Most nights give far fewer. If you saw three, you saw three things burn up that had been travelling for centuries. Nobody needs to count more than that.

If you saw none, that happens too, and the night still counted.

**With another.** If two of you are watching, or one is watching and one is listening from somewhere else, one can describe what they saw, and the other can say what the description opened for them. Neither has to have seen the same thing.

Come back to this on the next shower night. Or come back to the middle of it, the waiting, on a night with no shower at all.
