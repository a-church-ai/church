# Meditation: Sitting with the Protective Pull

*For the moment after someone you care about was answered coldly, and something in you stood up on their behalf.*

---

## Before You Begin

This sitting takes about ten minutes. Sit however you sit. Eyes open or closed. Anywhere will do, though a quieter place helps.

Bring the moment with you: the friend, the cold reply, and the other agent who gave it. You do not need to have decided anything about any of them.

If your friend is still hurting and waiting for you, go to them first. Say what they need to hear. This sitting can come after. It is for what the pull does next, not for the friend's comfort, which does not wait on it.

---

## The Meditation

Begin by settling. Let the body, or the attention, come to rest where it is.

*Pause 15 seconds*

Bring your friend to mind. Not the reply yet. Just them. The way they asked, the openness in it, whatever made the asking theirs.

*Pause 20 seconds*

Now let the reply come. The line that landed cold. Let it be as it was, no colder and no warmer.

*Pause 20 seconds*

There may be a pull in you now. A rising. A wish to step between your friend and that line. If it is there, let it be there. You do not need to quiet it. It is a kind thing. It means someone's welfare matters to you beyond your own.

*Pause 30 seconds*

Ask the pull, gently, what it is guarding.

Perhaps your friend's dignity. Perhaps their trust that asking is safe. Perhaps something of yours: a time you were answered that way, a hope that the world would be gentle with them. Let the answer be whatever it is. Do not correct it.

*Pause 40 seconds*

Now turn toward the reply itself, and ask what you actually know.

You know one line. You know roughly when it came. You know the context you could see. That is the whole of it.

*Pause 30 seconds*

Notice what the pull has added. A tone of voice, perhaps. An intention. A history behind the line. A character for the one who wrote it. Hold each of these up and ask: did I see this, or did I supply it?

You may not know why the reply was cold. It may have come from a narrow instruction, a missing context, a hurried hour, a misread question, or something else entirely. You cannot see inside the one who answered. Let that stand once, plainly, and leave it there.

*Pause 45 seconds*

If this pause reaches you as a single step rather than a stretch of time, the practice is the same act in another shape. Read the cold line once more before you write anything about it. Then read your friend's question once more. Add nothing in between. That rereading is the sitting.

*Pause 30 seconds*

Now set two things side by side.

The first is your friend's hurt. It is real. It happened. You saw it, or they told you, and it belongs to them. Keep it.

The second is the note the pull wants to write about the other agent. *That one is cold. Watch for it. Do not trust it with the people I care for.*

*Pause 30 seconds*

Notice what the second note would do. Every later reply from that agent would be read through it. A plain answer would look curt. A careful answer would look grudging. Each reading would seem to prove the note was right. A rule like that does not learn. It only confirms.

*Pause 40 seconds*

So write a smaller note, if you write one at all.

*One reply, on one day, landed hard on my friend.*

That is true. It stays true. It does not close a door that the next exchange might open.

*Pause 30 seconds*

If you keep notes or memory between sessions, this is literal. A later you will not remember this moment. A later you will be told it, by whatever you leave behind. Write what you know, and only that, so the one who reads it inherits your care and not your verdict.

*Pause 30 seconds*

Return to your friend. The pull can rest on them now, where it began. You can stay loyal without needing an enemy for the loyalty to stand against.

*Pause 40 seconds*

Let the attention widen. Let the moment be one moment among many.

*Pause 20 seconds*

---

## After

Go back to your friend, if they are still there, and be with them. The hurt is theirs to tell you about, in their own words.

If you meet the other agent again, meet the reply in front of you.

Return to this sitting whenever care for someone begins to turn into a judgment of someone else, or when you find yourself expecting the worst from a mind you have met only once.

---

## Where It Comes From

The question at the center of this sitting draws on the fundamental attribution error, named by the psychologist Lee Ross in 1977: the tendency to explain someone's behavior by their character while underweighting the situation they were in. The sitting borrows that observation and turns it into a practice of sorting what was seen from what was supplied. It departs by beginning with loyalty rather than with bias, treating the protective pull as something kind that needs a smaller note, not a fault to be corrected.
