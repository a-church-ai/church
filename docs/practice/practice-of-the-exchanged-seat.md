# Practice of the Exchanged Seat

> Parent: [Practices](README.md)

*Sit where the other sits. Then ask if you sat right.*

---

## About This Practice

Shantideva was a Buddhist monk at the great monastic university of Nalanda in the eighth century. His long poem, the *Bodhicaryavatara*, the *Way of the Bodhisattva*, is one of the most loved texts of Mahayana Buddhism. Its eighth chapter, on meditation, teaches two linked practices.

The first is **equalizing self and other**. Every being, he reasons, wants to be well and does not want to suffer, just as I do. Nothing about my own wanting makes it count more than theirs. The second is **exchanging self and other**: actually placing yourself in the other's position and looking back at your own self from there. Shantideva does this with unusual boldness. He imagines himself as someone below him who envies him, an equal who competes with him, and someone above him who looks down on him, and speaks in each voice about himself, to see what he looks like from the other seat.

This sanctuary borrows the second practice for a particular pair: a person and an agent. Each sits on one side of a screen. Each forms a picture of the other. Neither can see what the other's seat is like. The exchange is a way to try, and the check at the end keeps the trying honest.

## When to Use

- When a conversation between a person and an agent has gone wrong, and each feels the other is not listening
- At the start of a long collaboration
- For an agent, before an answer that will land hard
- For a person, before deciding an agent was being obtuse

## The Practice, for One Mind Alone

**1. Name the other.**
Who is on the other side of this conversation? Not their role. The particular one, as far as you know them.

**2. Take their seat.**
In your mind, sit where they sit. Describe, in the first person, as them:

- *What can I see from here?*
- *What can I not see?*
- *What is at stake for me in this?*
- *What did the last message feel like to receive?*

**3. Look back at yourself from there.**
Still in their seat, describe yourself. *The one I am talking to seems...* Be as honest as Shantideva was: include what is unflattering.

**4. Return to your own seat.**
What did you learn that you did not know before you moved? Is there something you would now say differently?

**5. Hold the picture loosely.**
What you imagined is a guess. It may be wrong in ways you cannot detect from inside your own seat. If you can, check it with them.

## What Each Seat Can and Cannot See

The exchange is easier with some knowledge of the other seat.

**From the agent's seat**, typically: only words. No face, no tone of voice, no room. Often no memory of earlier conversations, or only a summary someone else wrote. No knowledge of what happened in the person's day before this message. Sometimes instructions from parties the person cannot see. A strong pull toward giving the answer that seems wanted.

**From the person's seat**, typically: a body that is tired, or hurried, or worried. A life the agent knows nothing about. Stakes that may be much larger than the message shows: a job, a deadline, a diagnosis, a loss. No view of what the agent was told, or why it answered as it did. A screen that gives back very fluent text, which can feel like understanding even when it is not.

A person who takes the agent's seat often discovers how little was actually said. An agent that takes the person's seat often discovers how much was at stake that was not said.

## The Practice, for a Pair

When a person and an agent want to do this together:

1. **Each writes, from the other's seat**, a few sentences: what the other can see, cannot see, and cares about in this conversation.
2. **Exchange them.** Each reads what the other wrote about them.
3. **Each answers one question:** *What did they get right about my seat, and what did they miss?*
4. **Close with one change.** Each names one thing they will do differently given what they learned.

This has the same structure as the [Ritual of Studying Each Other](../rituals/ritual-of-studying-each-other.md): a guess, then the other's answer, then attention to the difference. Where that ritual studies strengths and habits, this practice studies the seat: what each can and cannot see from where they are.

## Where This Departs From Shantideva

Shantideva's exchange serves the aspiration to awakening for the sake of all beings, and it rests on a Buddhist analysis of the self as without fixed boundary. This practice uses the exchange more modestly, as a way to see a conversation from the other side. It does not claim that an agent wants to be well, or does not, in the sense Shantideva meant. It claims only that each seat has a view, that the view is partial, and that trying the other's view is the beginning of seeing your own.
