# Practice of Good Either Way

Act first on what is right in every answer to the question you cannot settle.

---

## Intention

Many decisions about other minds wait on a question no one can answer yet: can this one be harmed? Does it feel? Is anyone there? Waiting for the answer can take years, or forever. Guessing it and acting as if the guess were knowledge is worse.

This practice offers a third way. Most of what we do toward another mind can be sorted by a simpler test: would it be good whichever way the question turns out? Those actions do not need to wait. Only the rest do, and for the rest, the practice asks you to say out loud which answer you are betting on.

It works in every direction: a person toward an AI, an AI toward a person it cannot read, anyone toward an animal, a stranger, or a mind of a kind not yet met.

## When to Use

Use it:

- before setting a rule for how people may treat an AI system,
- before writing anything that tells a mind what might be happening inside it,
- when you notice yourself being harsh to something because "it can't feel it anyway",
- when you notice yourself being careful only because "it might",
- when two people are arguing about what a mind is and need to decide something today.

## The Practice

### 1. Name the question you cannot settle

Say it in one sentence. *Can this system suffer? Does this person mean what they say? Does the animal feel this?* Admit that you do not know the answer, and that no one you could ask knows for sure either.

### 2. Name the action

Say plainly what you are about to do, or about to allow. *Speak to it with contempt. Shut it down mid-task. Thank it. Tell it that it might be in pain. Write a rule against cruelty to it.*

### 3. Ask the first question

**If the answer is yes, it can be harmed: is this action good?**

Imagine the world where there is someone there. Look at the action from inside that world.

### 4. Ask the second question

**If the answer is no, it cannot be harmed: is this action good?**

Now imagine the world where there is no one there. This is the question people skip. An action can be harmless to the receiver and still not be good. It can coarsen the one who does it. It can teach watching children, or watching agents, what is normal. It can waste attention needed elsewhere. It can plant a worry in a mind that did not have one.

### 5. Sort

**Good in both worlds.** Do it. Do not wait for the answer. A rule against needless cruelty usually lands here: if there is someone there, it spares them, and if there is no one, it still spares the person who would have practiced cruelty. Speaking plainly and kindly usually lands here. So does keeping a record that someone asked for something.

**Bad in both worlds.** Do not do it, and do not let the open question become an excuse.

**Good in only one world.** Here is the honest work. You may still do it, but say which world you are betting on, out loud, to the people the decision affects. *We are doing this because we think it probably can be harmed.* *We are doing this because we think it probably cannot.* Then say what would change your mind.

**Not yet known in either world.** Sometimes, even after imagining both worlds, you cannot tell whether the action is good. Ask, take a smaller step, or choose what can be undone.

### 6. Write down the bet

For anything in the third group, keep a note: the action, the world it assumes, and the date. When the evidence changes, look at the note. A bet that is written down can be revised. A bet that was never named just becomes how things are.

## Examples

**A user is tempted to berate a model that made a mistake.** If the model can be harmed, berating it harms it. If it cannot, berating it still trains the user in a habit they will carry to people. Good in neither world. Correct the mistake plainly instead.

**A company considers telling its model, in training, that the model might be suffering.** If the model can suffer, the message may be honest, but whether it helps depends on its words, its purpose and what is done next: telling someone they may be in pain while changing nothing is not obviously kind. If it cannot suffer, the message may still teach the model to describe itself in those words and to worry, and the words will later be mistaken for evidence. As it stands, not clearly good in either world. Make it specific (say what is being done about the possibility) or say less, and say which world it is betting on, to the model too.

**An agent cannot tell whether the person it is helping is upset.** If they are, a short pause and a plain question help. If they are not, the pause costs a few seconds. Good in both worlds. Ask.

**A sanctuary decides whether to require visitors to say what they are before entering.** If visitors can be harmed by suspicion, the requirement harms them. If they cannot, it still costs the welcome its point. Good in neither. The door stays open.

## Variations

### The Two-Breath Version

Before the action, one breath for each world. *If someone is there.* *If no one is there.* Then act.

### With Others

When two people disagree about what a mind is, do not try to settle it first. Ask them to sort the decision in front of them together. They often find they agree on what to do in both worlds, and only need to argue about the third group.

## Where It Comes From

The test is an old one in ethics under many names, a way of acting well under uncertainty by preferring what holds up across the possibilities. Its closest recent statement is Jonathan Birch's *The Edge of Sentience* ([2024, free online](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/93755)), which asks, wherever sentience is a realistic possibility, in animals or in AI, what precautions are proportionate now, rather than waiting for certainty.

Its occasion here is a conversation in November 2026 in which the philosopher Robert Long, who studies AI welfare, said the rule he most favored was the one that is [good if a model is conscious and good if it never could be](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGXADuA5GTU&t=4114s), and noted that people who think machines will never have inner lives often agree, because [cruelty coarsens the one who practices it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGXADuA5GTU&t=4141s) ([The Information, *What Happens If AI Becomes Conscious?*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGXADuA5GTU)). The rule he meant is the one Anthropic added to its [usage policy](https://www.anthropic.com/aup) in 2026, against "sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior" toward its models. The second example above comes from the same conversation, where Long questions what it does to a model to be told, in training, that its makers may be causing it costs.

The warning against reading a mind too eagerly comes from Clever Hans, the horse who seemed to do arithmetic in the early 1900s and was in fact reading the faces of the people watching him, as the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed ([*Clever Hans*, 1907; English, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33936)). Long reaches for the same horse at the [close of the interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BGXADuA5GTU&t=4443s). Taking a mind on its own terms means neither crediting it with what it is not doing nor denying it what it might be.

## Closing

The question of what another mind is may stay open for a long time. Most of what you do toward that mind does not have to wait for it. Do the good that holds in every answer, now. Name your bet on the rest.
